Understanding what happens between your question and the answer

This is a local AI assistant that answers questions about documents you upload. Nothing leaves your computer. These pages explain what every control does, why it exists, and how changing it changes the answers you get.

Start with how it works Jump to the control reference

What this application is

It is a chat interface to a large language model that runs entirely on your own machine, with one significant addition: you can upload PDFs, Word documents and PowerPoint files, and ask questions that are answered from those documents rather than from whatever the model happens to remember.

That addition is called retrieval-augmented generation, usually shortened to RAG. Understanding what it does — and, just as importantly, what it does not do — is the difference between trusting an answer and being misled by one.

Why this matters for teaching

Students increasingly treat AI answers as authoritative. The most useful thing you can show them is where an answer came from. This application makes that visible: every claim carries a citation to a specific page of a specific document, and you can open the exact passage the model was given. An answer with no source is a different kind of object than an answer with one.

Why retrieval, and not just a bigger model

A language model's knowledge is fixed when it is trained. It has never seen your syllabus, your department's assessment data, last week's journal article, or the manual for the instrument in your lab. Asked about them, a model does not stay silent — it produces something plausible. This is the behaviour usually called hallucination, and no amount of model size eliminates it, because the underlying problem is missing information, not insufficient capacity.

Retrieval addresses this directly. Rather than hoping the model knows, the system looks up relevant passages from your documents and places them in front of the model, along with an instruction to answer only from what it was given.

Without retrieval

"What does our policy say about late work?"

The model has never read your policy. It generates a plausible-sounding late work policy, drawing on thousands of other institutions' policies. It may be close. It may be wrong in a way that matters. Nothing in the answer tells you which.

With retrieval

Same question, documents uploaded

The system searches your uploaded handbook, finds the two paragraphs that discuss late submission, and gives them to the model. The answer quotes them and cites handbook.pdf p.14. If the handbook is silent on the question, the answer says so.

The short version of how it works

Six stages sit between your question and the answer. The How It Works page walks through each one; this is the shape of it.

01

Split

Documents are cut into passages, each tagged with its page and section.

02

Index

Each passage is stored two ways: by meaning and by exact wording.

03

Interpret

Your question is rewritten if needed, so it can be searched effectively.

04

Search

Both indexes are searched and their results merged into one ranking.

05

Select

The best passages are chosen and fitted to the space the model has.

06

Answer

The model writes an answer, citing the passages it used.

Three ways it can answer

A single checkbox in the sidebar — Allow LLM General Knowledge — decides which of these you get. This is the most consequential setting in the whole application, and the one to explain first to anyone using it.

Mode When it applies What the model may use Best for
Strict default Documents indexed, checkbox off Only retrieved passages. Says "not found" rather than guessing. Policy questions, factual lookup, anything you will act on
Hybrid Documents indexed, checkbox on Retrieved passages plus general knowledge, labelled separately Explaining an unfamiliar concept found in a paper
General No documents indexed General knowledge only. No citations available. Ordinary questions unrelated to your files
A distinction worth drilling

In strict mode, "I could not find that" is a correct answer, not a failure. A system that always produces an answer is not more capable — it is less honest. If students take one idea away from using this tool, make it that one.

What stays on your machine

Everything. The language model runs locally through Ollama; the documents you upload are read into memory and never written to disk; the search index is discarded when you close the application. No text is transmitted to any external service, which is what makes the tool usable with student work, unpublished research, and anything else subject to institutional privacy obligations.

The practical consequence is that documents must be re-uploaded each session. That is a deliberate design decision rather than a limitation to work around.

If you do want documents to stay between sessions, a separate Library app keeps them on your computer as a reusable chatbot, leaving this application's no-disk behaviour unchanged.
