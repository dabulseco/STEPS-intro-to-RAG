Concepts

How It Works

Six stages sit between your question and the answer. Each one can be adjusted, and each has a failure mode worth recognising.

Stage 1 — Splitting documents into passages

A language model can only consider a limited amount of text at once. A 200-page manual will not fit, so documents are cut into chunks: passages of roughly 400 words.

How you cut matters. Split carelessly and a sentence is severed mid-clause, leaving two fragments that mean less than the whole. This application cuts at paragraph boundaries where possible and never allows a chunk to span two pages — which is what makes it possible to say an answer came from page 14 and mean it.

The idea underneath

Two sizes are kept for every passage. A small version is used for searching, because a short passage is about one thing and therefore matches precisely. A larger surrounding version is what the model actually reads, so it sees the full argument rather than a fragment. Search small, read big.

Stage 2 — Indexing, twice

Each passage is then stored in two separate indexes. This is the single most important design decision in the system, and the one most worth explaining.

Index 1 · Semantic

Search by meaning

Each passage is converted into a list of numbers — an embedding — that represents its meaning. Passages about similar ideas end up with similar numbers, even when they share no words.

Finds: "how warm were the cells kept" → "cultured at 37 °C"
Misses: catalogue number ZZQX7-4419, which means nothing

Index 2 · Keyword

Search by exact wording

A traditional word index, the same principle a library catalogue uses. It counts which passages contain your terms and how unusual those terms are.

Finds: ZZQX7-4419, CFTR-KO, "Title IX"
Misses: a paraphrase sharing none of your words

Each index fails exactly where the other succeeds. Running both and combining them is called hybrid search, and it is why this application handles both "explain the general argument" and "find part number 4419" without you having to choose in advance.

Stage 3 — Interpreting your question

The words you type are aimed at a human reader, not at a search index. Consider a perfectly ordinary follow-up question:

"How does that compare to the control?"

Searched literally, this is nearly useless. The subject lives in the previous exchange; what remains is grammar. The application therefore rewrites it into a standalone question — "How does the 38 nA/cm² chloride transport in the CFTR-KO line compare to the wildtype control?" — before any searching happens.

This is on by default. Several further rewriting strategies are available and off by default; see Advanced Techniques.

Stage 4 — Searching and merging

Both indexes are searched. Each returns its own ranked list, and the two lists must be combined into one. The difficulty is that their scores are not comparable — a similarity score and a keyword score are different kinds of number on different scales.

The solution is to ignore the scores and use only the positions. A passage ranked first by either method scores well; a passage ranked first by both scores best of all. This is called Reciprocal Rank Fusion, and its advantage is that it needs no tuning for your particular documents.

What you can adjust

The Semantic ↔ Keyword balance slider weights the two sides. Move it toward keyword when exact terms matter, toward semantic for conceptual questions. Fifty-fifty is a good default and rarely a bad one.

Stage 5 — Selecting what fits

Retrieval deliberately returns more passages than needed — around twenty candidates — because it is better to over-collect and then filter. Two steps narrow them down.

Re-ranking (optional) takes the survivors and scores each one against your question directly, keeping the best five. Retrieval optimises for finding everything relevant; re-ranking optimises for putting the best thing first. They want different things, which is why doing both beats doing either harder.

Budgeting then fits the chosen passages into the space the model has, measured from the Context Length setting. If something does not fit, the application reports it in the diagnostics panel rather than dropping it silently.

The failure this prevents

Systems that paste entire documents into the prompt hit the model's limit and have the excess silently discarded. No error appears. The model answers from whatever fragment survived and sounds equally confident either way. If you evaluate another RAG tool, ask what it does when a document exceeds the context window — the answer tells you a great deal.

Stage 6 — Answering, with sources

The selected passages are numbered [S1], [S2] and given to the model with instructions to cite them. The answer arrives with those markers inline, and the Sources panel beneath it shows each passage in full, with its file, page and section.

The citations are then checked. Every marker is verified against the passages actually supplied; if the model invents a reference to [S7] when only five passages were provided, a warning appears above the answer. Asking a model to cite its sources is easy — verifying that it did is what makes the citation worth anything.

Reading the diagnostics panel

Every answer carries an expandable Retrieval Diagnostics panel. It exists to answer one question: when an answer is disappointing, was the problem finding the information or writing about it?

ReadingInterpretationWhat to do
Sources used: 0 Nothing was found. The model never had a chance. Rephrase, adjust the balance slider, or check the document indexed correctly
Semantic hits high, keyword 0 Your terms do not appear literally in the documents Normal for paraphrased questions; nothing to fix
Passages dropped for budget More was found than would fit in the context window Raise Context Length
Sources look right, answer is poor Retrieval worked; generation did not Lower Temperature, or use a larger chat model
A classroom exercise

Ask a question whose answer is genuinely absent from the uploaded documents, in strict mode. The system should decline. Then enable Allow LLM General Knowledge and ask again — now it will answer from training data. Putting those two responses side by side makes the distinction between grounded and ungrounded output concrete in a way that explaining it rarely does.
