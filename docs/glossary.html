Glossary

The vocabulary used across these pages, in plain language. Written to be usable as a handout.

Terms

Large Language Model (LLM)

A system trained to predict what text comes next, on enough material that doing so well requires something resembling understanding. It has no memory between conversations and no access to anything beyond its training data unless you supply it.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

Searching your documents for relevant passages and giving them to the model along with the question, so its answer is grounded in your material rather than its training data. The "retrieval" half is a search engine; the "generation" half is the language model. Both must work for the answer to be trustworthy.

Hallucination

Fluent, confident output that is not true. It is not a malfunction — the model is doing exactly what it was built to do, producing plausible text. Retrieval reduces it by supplying real information; citations make it detectable by letting you check.

Grounding

Tying an answer to specific supplied evidence. A grounded answer can be checked against a source; an ungrounded one can only be believed or doubted. The distinction is the single most useful idea to convey to students.

Chunk

A passage a document has been cut into — here, roughly 400 words. Chunks are the unit that gets searched and retrieved. This application never lets one span two pages, which is what makes page citations exact.

Embedding

A list of numbers representing the meaning of a piece of text. Passages about similar ideas produce similar numbers even when they share no words, which is what allows searching by meaning rather than by wording.

Vector store

The index holding those numeric representations, able to find the passages closest in meaning to a question. In this application it lives in memory only and is discarded when you close the app.

Semantic search & keyword search

Semantic finds passages that mean the same thing as your question, even in different words. Keyword finds passages containing your exact terms. Each misses what the other catches, which is why this application runs both.

Hybrid search & rank fusion

Running both search methods and merging their results. Because their scores are not comparable, the merge uses rank positions instead — a passage ranked highly by either method scores well, and one ranked highly by both scores best.

BM25

The standard keyword-ranking method, used by search engines and library catalogues for decades. It weighs how often your terms appear in a passage against how common those terms are overall, so rare words count for more.

Re-ranking

A second pass that re-scores retrieved passages against the question and reorders them. Search casts a wide net; re-ranking decides what is genuinely best within the catch.

Metadata & pre-filtering

Metadata is what a passage carries besides its text — file name, page, section. Pre-filtering uses it to narrow the search before it runs, for example to a single document.

HyDE

Hypothetical Document Embeddings. The model invents a plausible answer and that invented text is used as the search key, because answers resemble document passages more closely than questions do. The invention is never shown to you and never used as evidence.

Token

The unit models process — roughly ¾ of a word. Common words are one token; unusual ones split into several. Context windows are measured in tokens, which is why a limit of 8,192 does not correspond neatly to a page count.

Context window

The total amount of text a model can consider at once, shared between instructions, conversation history, retrieved passages, your question and the answer. Exceeding it means something gets dropped — in poorly built systems, silently.

Temperature

How strictly the model favours its most likely next word. Low values give focused, repeatable text; high values give varied, less predictable text. For document questions, low.

Top-K & Top-P

Two further limits on which words the model may choose from. Top-K caps the number of candidates; Top-P keeps the smallest group of candidates whose combined probability reaches a threshold. Both overlap with temperature — adjust temperature and leave these alone.

System prompt

Standing instructions given to the model before the conversation begins — here, who it is writing for and whether it may use knowledge beyond your documents. Rules placed here hold for the whole exchange rather than fading as the conversation grows.

Local / on-device

The model runs on your own computer rather than a company's servers. Slower than commercial services and typically less capable, but nothing you type or upload leaves the machine — which is what makes it appropriate for student work and unpublished material.

Ephemeral index

A search index that exists only while the application is running. Documents must be re-processed each session — a deliberate privacy choice rather than a limitation, since nothing is ever written to disk.

Citation verification

Checking that every source marker in an answer refers to a passage that was actually supplied. Asking a model to cite is easy; confirming it did is what makes the citation mean anything.

Five points for a one-page handout

  1. An AI answer is not a source. It is a summary of something, and the question is always: of what?
  2. "I could not find that" is a good answer. A system that always answers is not more capable, only less honest.
  3. Check one citation. Open the cited page and confirm it says what the answer claims. It takes seconds and it is the whole skill.
  4. Retrieval and writing fail differently. A wrong answer can mean the system searched badly or wrote badly, and the fixes are unrelated.
  5. Settings are choices, not physics. Someone picked the temperature, the number of passages, the search balance. Different choices give different answers to the same question.
